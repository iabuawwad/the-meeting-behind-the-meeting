# It’s the Way You Say It  
## Reference Guide, Frameworks, Key Takeaways, Techniques, and Practice Manual

**Book:** *It’s the Way You Say It: Becoming Articulate, Well-Spoken, and Clear*  
**Author:** Carol A. Fleming, PhD  
**Purpose of this guide:** Convert the book into a practical reference for improving voice, speech clarity, articulation, verbal confidence, nonverbal alignment, business communication, small talk, interviews, and presentations.

---

## 1. Core Thesis

Professional communication is not only about what you say. It is also about how you say it.

Your voice, pacing, articulation, posture, facial expression, eye contact, and word choice shape how others judge your confidence, competence, maturity, warmth, clarity, and authority.

The book’s main argument:

> Your voice is not fixed.  
> Your speaking habits can be assessed, trained, practised, and improved.

The author treats communication as a whole-person behaviour. Body, words, and voice must ideally communicate the same message at the same time for a speaker to appear professional, trustworthy, clear, and appealing.

---

## 2. What the Book Is Really Providing

This book provides a practical speech improvement system.

It helps readers:

- Hear themselves more objectively
- Identify speech and voice habits that weaken their message
- Correct specific voice problems
- Build a more dynamic and resonant voice
- Become more articulate and concise
- Align verbal and nonverbal communication
- Improve business introductions, interviews, voicemail, small talk, and presentations
- Practise deliberately until new patterns become natural

The book is not only about public speaking. It is about everyday professional presence.

---

## 3. Foundational Principle: You Do Not Hear Yourself Accurately

A major idea in the book is that people are poor judges of their own voice.

You hear your voice internally through your own head and body. Other people hear the external sound waves. This creates a mismatch between how you think you sound and how others actually hear you.

Also, when speaking, your brain is busy forming meaning, choosing words, managing emotion, and responding socially. You usually do not have enough attention left to judge your own sound accurately.

## Practical implication

You need external feedback.

Use:

- Recordings
- Playback
- Trusted listeners
- Professional coaching when needed
- Objective checklists
- Repeated practice

---

## 4. The Communication Improvement Cycle

Use this cycle for any speech or voice issue.

### Step 1: Record yourself

Capture natural speech, not only rehearsed reading.

### Step 2: Listen objectively

Listen for:

- Speed
- Volume
- Pitch
- Clarity
- Energy
- Pauses
- Fading endings
- Mumbling
- Tone
- Confidence
- Warmth

### Step 3: Identify one priority problem

Do not try to fix everything at once.

Pick one issue:

- Too fast
- Too soft
- Too loud
- Raspy voice
- High voice
- Indecisive tone
- Staccato rhythm
- Breathy voice
- Fading endings
- Weak articulation

### Step 4: Practise deliberately

Use short, focused practice sessions several times a day.

The book’s approach favours frequent short practice over long unfocused sessions.

### Step 5: Apply in low-risk situations

Practise with neutral listeners:

- Store clerk
- Receptionist
- Phone inquiry
- Restaurant order
- Short casual exchange

### Step 6: Carry over into real conversations

Use the new habit in work conversations, meetings, calls, interviews, and presentations.

### Step 7: Repeat until natural

New speech behaviours feel strange at first. Strange does not mean wrong. It usually means new.

---

## 5. Deliberate Practice Rules

Speech change requires deliberate practice.

## What works

- Short sessions
- Full attention
- Repetition
- Recording
- Playback
- Specific goals
- Gradual increase in difficulty
- Real-world carryover
- Feedback from trusted listeners

## What fails

- Vague intention
- Practising once or twice
- Trying to fix everything
- Practising while distracted
- Practising only in your head
- Expecting immediate comfort
- Assuming “strange” means “bad”

## Practical standard

Practise 3 to 4 times per day for 6 to 12 minutes.

The practice must be mindful. Driving, watching TV, or multitasking does not count.

# PART I: ASSESSING YOUR VOICE

---

## 6. What Counts as a Speech Problem?

A problem is any feature of your speech that calls attention to itself or distracts listeners from your message.

Examples:

- Speech is too fast to follow
- Voice is too soft to hear
- Voice is too loud for the room
- Voice sounds raspy, strained, or unpleasant
- Pitch sounds too high or too low for the context
- Sentences sound unsure
- Words are clipped
- Breath runs out
- Endings fade
- Articulation is unclear
- Nonverbal behaviour contradicts the message

The standard is not perfection. The standard is effective communication.

---

## 7. Self-Assessment Checklist

Ask yourself:

- Do people often ask me to repeat myself?
- Do people interrupt because they misunderstand?
- Do I sound younger or less mature than I want?
- Do I speak too fast when excited?
- Do my sentences fade at the end?
- Do I sound unsure even when I am certain?
- Do I avoid phone calls, meetings, presentations, or small talk?
- Do I dislike hearing my recorded voice?
- Do others comment on my loudness, softness, accent, pitch, or clarity?
- Does my voice become tired, sore, or raspy?
- Does my body language support my message?

---

## 8. External Feedback Questions

Ask trusted listeners:

- What three words describe how I sound?
- Am I easy to understand?
- Do I speak too fast, too slowly, too softly, or too loudly?
- Do I sound confident?
- Do I sound warm?
- Do I sound professional?
- Do I sound rushed?
- Do I sound clear?
- Do I sound engaged?
- What distracts from my message?

Instruction to listeners:

> Be specific. Do not say “good” or “bad.” Describe what you hear.

# PART II: SPECIFIC SPEECH AND VOICE PROBLEMS

---

## 9. Fast Talkers

## Problem

Fast talkers speak at a pace that feels normal to them but is difficult for listeners to process.

Fast speech can cause:

- Slurred articulation
- Lost words
- Weak pauses
- Reduced authority
- Listener fatigue
- Perceived nervousness
- Reduced clarity in technical topics

## Target rate

For normal conversation, aim roughly around 155 to 175 words per minute.

For technical, formal, noisy, or large-room settings, slow down further.

## Why fast speech happens

- High urgency
- Anxiety
- Excitement
- Habit
- Family or cultural style
- Fear of interruption
- Desire to finish quickly
- Thinking faster than speaking

## Correction techniques

- Record a 160-word passage and time it for about one minute.
- Practise reading at a controlled pace.
- Use pauses deliberately.
- Speak in shorter thought groups.
- Record voicemail to yourself and listen.
- Practise with neutral listeners.
- Slow down more when the content is technical or new to the audience.
- When emotional, consciously return to your controlled reading pace.

## Executive rule

Speed is not intelligence. Clarity is.

---

## 10. Loud Talkers

## Problem

Loud talkers may sound aggressive, unaware, overwhelming, or emotionally uncontrolled.

The issue is often subjective, but listeners may physically pull back, avoid engagement, or close themselves off.

## Why it happens

- High energy
- Emotional intensity
- Habit
- Poor self-awareness
- Hearing issues
- Environment with loud models
- Need to dominate
- Excitement

## Correction techniques

- Use recording or sound level feedback.
- Ask for agreed signals from trusted colleagues.
- Notice when listeners step back.
- Learn how “too loud” feels physically.
- Practise lowering volume without losing energy.
- Check hearing if volume control is consistently difficult.

## Leadership risk

A loud voice can unintentionally create fear, resistance, or avoidance.

---

## 11. Soft Talkers

## Problem

Soft talkers may be hard to hear, especially in meetings, presentations, conference rooms, or noisy settings.

Being unheard weakens authority.

## Causes

- Shyness
- Lack of preparation
- Low confidence
- Intimate conversational style
- Poor breath support
- Weak projection habit
- Reluctance to claim space

## Correction framework

Soft voice improvement requires:

1. Motivation
2. Energy
3. Air

## 11.1 Motivation

You must want to be heard.

Preparation improves motivation. When you know your material, you are more willing to project it.

## 11.2 Energy

Practise projecting your voice across a room.

Exercise:

- Place photos or faces at the back of a room.
- Speak to each face for five seconds.
- Deliver a complete thought before moving to the next face.
- Practise with real people at the back of the room.

## 11.3 Air

Projection requires breath support.

Principles:

- Relax shoulders
- Keep chest open
- Breathe into the belly and midsection
- Avoid throat strain
- Breathe before speaking
- Give yourself time for full inhalation

## Executive rule

Do not confuse calmness with smallness. A calm voice can still command the room.

---

## 12. Raspy Talkers

## Problem

A raspy, gravelly, or hoarse voice may sound strained, tired, unhealthy, or unclear.

It can come from habit or medical issues.

## Important warning

If raspiness is persistent, painful, or unexplained, consult a doctor or voice specialist.

## Causes

- Voice held back in the throat
- Inadequate breath support
- Habitual low pitch
- Vocal abuse
- Throat tension
- Medical condition
- Overuse
- Trying to sound tougher or less feminine/masculine
- Speaking in noisy environments

## Correction techniques

- Move voice forward toward the lips and front of the face.
- Practise front sounds such as consonants formed near the lips.
- Record and compare throat placement versus forward placement.
- Hydrate before heavy speaking.
- Avoid yelling, excessive throat clearing, and loud talking in noise.
- Rest the voice when strained.
- Use breath support instead of throat force.

## Practical cue

Think:

> Send the sound from the lips to the listener.

---

## 13. High Talkers

## Problem

A high-pitched voice may cause a speaker to be perceived as young, anxious, immature, or less authoritative.

The goal is not to fake a deep voice. The goal is to find a comfortable, natural, mature pitch.

## Causes

- Habit
- Anxiety
- Self-image
- Trying to sound pleasant
- Lack of vocal grounding
- Tension
- Physical voice characteristics

## Correction techniques

- Get external feedback from trusted listeners.
- Use “um-hum” responses to identify natural pitch.
- Avoid pushing artificially low.
- Find a comfortable pitch with room below it for variation.
- Practise relaxed vocal placement.
- Use breath support.
- Record and listen for naturalness.

## Rule

Authority does not come from forcing a low voice. It comes from a supported, resonant, steady voice.

---

## 14. Indecisive Talkers

## Problem

Indecisive talkers sound uncertain because of vocal patterns rather than actual uncertainty.

Common markers:

- Rising intonation at the end of statements
- Excessive fillers
- Hesitation
- Weak sentence endings
- Overqualification
- Lack of declarative tone

## Listener impression

- Unsure
- Needing approval
- Weak authority
- Unprepared
- Tentative

## Correction techniques

- Use declarative sentence endings.
- Practise downward inflection for statements.
- Reduce unnecessary qualifiers.
- Record statements and check whether they sound like questions.
- Use shorter sentences.
- Pause instead of filling.
- State conclusions directly.

## Better pattern

Weak:

> “I think maybe we should probably review this?”

Strong:

> “We should review this before approval.”

---

## 15. Staccato Talkers

## Problem

Staccato speech sounds choppy, abrupt, clipped, or disconnected.

It may weaken warmth and flow.

## Causes

- Tension
- Overcontrol
- Anxiety
- Habit
- Reading word by word
- Lack of phrasing
- Poor breath flow

## Correction techniques

- Speak in thought groups, not single words.
- Use phrases.
- Connect words naturally.
- Practise smooth reading aloud.
- Use breath to carry full phrases.
- Record and listen for flow.
- Mark phrase breaks in text.

## Example

Choppy:

> “We. Need. To. Review. This. Today.”

Natural:

> “We need to review this today.”

---

## 16. Breathy Talkers

## Problem

A breathy voice may sound weak, intimate, tired, overly soft, or lacking authority.

## Causes

- Too much air escaping
- Weak vocal fold closure
- Habit
- Desire to sound gentle
- Lack of energy
- Possible medical issue

## Correction techniques

- Increase vocal energy.
- Improve breath support.
- Practise clear onset of sound.
- Avoid whispery production.
- Record and compare breathy versus supported voice.
- Consult a professional if persistent.

## Rule

Warmth does not require breathiness. You can sound warm and still sound firm.

---

## 17. Fading Talkers

## Problem

Fading talkers lose energy at the end of phrases or sentences.

The result:

- Key words disappear
- Authority drops
- Listener misses the point
- Speech sounds unfinished
- Confidence appears weak

## Causes

- Running out of air
- Poor phrasing
- Habit
- Low commitment to sentence endings
- Anxiety
- Talking too long without breath

## Correction techniques

- Plan shorter phrases.
- Breathe before important points.
- Keep energy through the final word.
- Practise reading sentences with strong endings.
- Record and check whether final words are audible.
- Mark phrase endings in notes.

## Executive rule

The end of the sentence often carries the decision. Do not let it die.

# PART III: DEVELOPING A DYNAMIC VOICE

---

## 18. Dynamic Voice

A dynamic voice uses variation in:

- Rate
- Pitch
- Volume
- Pauses
- Emphasis
- Rhythm
- Resonance
- Emotional colour

A flat voice makes listeners work harder. A dynamic voice guides attention and helps meaning land.

---

## 19. Vocal Variety

Vocal variety helps listeners follow, interpret, and stay engaged.

## Components

### Rate

Speed of speaking.

Use faster rate for energy and familiar material. Use slower rate for complex, emotional, technical, or important material.

### Pause

The pause is one of the most powerful tools.

Use pauses to:

- Let meaning land
- Give listeners time to process
- Create emphasis
- Show confidence
- Reduce filler words
- Control pace

### Pitch

Pitch variation prevents monotony and communicates emotional meaning.

### Volume

Volume variation adds emphasis and energy, but must fit the room.

### Rhythm

Good speech has natural movement, not mechanical delivery.

---

## 20. Emphasis

Emphasis tells the listener what matters.

Without emphasis, all words have equal weight, and the listener must guess the meaning.

## Practice

Take a sentence and stress different words.

Example:

> “I did not approve that change.”

Different emphasis changes meaning:

- **I** did not approve that change.
- I did **not** approve that change.
- I did not approve **that** change.
- I did not approve that **change**.

## Executive use

Use emphasis to make decisions, risks, priorities, and accountability clear.

---

## 21. Resonant Voice

A resonant voice sounds full, supported, and easy to listen to.

It is not forced. It is supported.

## Benefits

- More authority
- Better audibility
- Less strain
- Warmer tone
- Stronger presence
- Better endurance

## Techniques

- Relax throat and shoulders.
- Use breath support.
- Keep sound forward.
- Practise humming.
- Feel vibration in the front of the face.
- Avoid pushing from the throat.
- Use open posture.

---

## 22. Pitch: Finding the Right Level

The goal is an appropriate pitch, not a fake low pitch.

## Practice

Use natural “um-hum” responses to find a comfortable pitch.

Then confirm:

- Does it feel relaxed?
- Is there room below it?
- Does it sound natural on recording?
- Do listeners hear it as mature and comfortable?

Avoid speaking at the very bottom of your range. That can create strain, gravel, and reduced expressiveness.

# PART IV: BECOMING WELL-SPOKEN

---

## 23. What “Well-Spoken” Means

Being well-spoken means speaking with:

- Clarity
- Fluency
- Courtesy
- Precision
- Confidence
- Appropriate vocabulary
- Good sentence structure
- Effective tone
- Listener awareness

It is not about sounding artificial or elite. It is about being easy to understand and worth listening to.

---

## 24. Simple Declarative Sentences

The book strongly recommends the simple declarative sentence for clear, forceful communication.

## Structure

Subject + predicate + period.

## Weak version

> “If possible, maybe we should think about reviewing this later because it might be useful.”

## Strong version

> “We need to review this today.”

## Use simple declarative sentences when

- Giving direction
- Making decisions
- Clarifying expectations
- Explaining risk
- Handling pressure
- Managing crisis
- Presenting executive summaries
- Stopping confusion

## Rule

Clear sentences create clear authority.

---

## 25. Avoid Empty Language

Empty language weakens authority.

Examples:

- “Kind of”
- “Sort of”
- “Like”
- “You know”
- “Basically”
- “Actually”
- “I guess”
- “Maybe”
- “Whatever”
- “Stuff”
- “Things”
- “At the end of the day” when overused
- Long preambles

## Replacement strategy

- Pause instead of filler.
- Use specific nouns.
- Use active verbs.
- Say the point directly.
- Stop when the sentence is complete.

---

## 26. Vocabulary Building

Vocabulary improves precision.

Two useful categories:

### Verbal vocabulary

Words you can use in speech naturally.

### Visual vocabulary

Words and images you recognise and understand but may not use in conversation.

## Practical methods

- Read widely.
- Collect useful phrases.
- Notice strong speakers.
- Look up unfamiliar words.
- Practise new words aloud.
- Use professional language without jargon overload.
- Prefer simple accuracy over complex display.

## Executive rule

The goal is not to sound complicated. The goal is to be exact.

---

## 27. Speaking Your Mind Effectively

There is a difference between self-expression and communication.

Self-expression is what you want to say.

Communication considers what the listener needs to understand.

## Effective communication requires

- Clear purpose
- Listener awareness
- Appropriate timing
- Specific language
- Controlled tone
- Respectful framing
- Confirmation of understanding

## Better question

Instead of:

> “What do I want to say?”

Ask:

> “What does this listener need to understand and do?”

---

## 28. Gracious Response

A gracious response shows respect and warmth.

It is essential for professional and social fluency.

## Examples

- “Thank you for raising that.”
- “That is helpful.”
- “I see your point.”
- “I appreciate the clarification.”
- “That must have taken effort.”
- “I had not considered that.”
- “You handled that well.”

## Why it matters

Graciousness makes conversation smoother, reduces defensiveness, and increases social trust.

It is not weakness. It is controlled interpersonal skill.

---

## 29. Articulation

Articulation is the clarity of speech sounds.

Problems include:

- Mumbling
- Dropped endings
- Omitted sounds
- Blended words
- Weak consonants
- Overly casual speech in professional settings

## Techniques

- Slow down.
- Over-articulate in practice.
- Record and listen.
- Practise difficult sounds.
- Keep lips and jaw active.
- Use clear consonant endings.
- Move from word practice to phrase practice to conversation.

## Rule

Clear speech does not mean stiff speech. It means complete speech.

# PART V: UNIFYING VERBAL AND NONVERBAL MESSAGES

---

## 30. The Alignment Principle

Your body, words, and voice must support each other.

Misalignment weakens credibility.

Examples:

- Saying “I am confident” while shrinking physically
- Giving good news with a flat face
- Offering support while looking away
- Claiming authority with fading voice
- Saying “I’m interested” while checking the phone

## Rule

People believe the total message, not the words alone.

---

## 31. Carrying Yourself with Confidence

Confidence is communicated through:

- Head carriage
- Posture
- Stillness
- Grounded stance
- Facial availability
- Eye contact
- Vocal steadiness
- Controlled gestures

## Head carriage

Keep the head balanced, not collapsed or pulled away.

## Posture

Use open, upright posture. Avoid slumping, shrinking, or excessive tension.

## Presence

Stillness can create authority. Nervous movement can distract.

---

## 32. How You Look When You Talk

Your face participates in speech.

Facial expression should match your meaning.

## Watch for

- Frozen expression
- Excessive tension
- Lack of warmth
- Inappropriate smiling
- Defensive face
- Blankness
- Over-animation

## Practical exercise

Record video while speaking.

Check:

- Do I look engaged?
- Does my face match my message?
- Do I look approachable?
- Do I look confident?
- Do I look too tense or too casual?

---

## 33. Eye Contact

Eye contact creates connection and credibility.

Poor eye contact can signal:

- Discomfort
- Lack of confidence
- Disinterest
- Evasion
- Overdependence on notes

Excessive eye contact can feel aggressive.

## Good practice

- Look at one person long enough to complete a thought.
- Move naturally to another listener.
- In presentations, include the back of the room.
- On video calls, look at the camera when making key points.
- Do not read continuously from notes.

---

## 34. Showing Interest

Interest is shown through:

- Facing the speaker
- Nodding naturally
- Leaning slightly forward
- Responsive facial expression
- Asking questions
- Removing distractions
- Verbal acknowledgements
- Relevant follow-up

Interest must be visible. Silent internal interest is not enough.

---

## 35. Approachability: SOFTEN

The book includes SOFTEN as an approachability framework.

## SOFTEN

| Letter | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| S | Smile |
| O | Open posture |
| F | Forward lean |
| T | Touch, where appropriate |
| E | Eye contact |
| N | Nod |

Use SOFTEN to appear more approachable, warm, and engaged.

## Caution

Apply context and culture. Touch is not always appropriate.

---

## 36. Height, Presence, and Message

Shorter speakers may need to be especially conscious of projection, posture, clothing, eye contact, and vocal authority.

The core principle is not height. It is presence.

Presence comes from:

- Clear voice
- Upright posture
- Direct language
- Grounded stance
- Strong eye contact
- Controlled pace
- Concise message

# PART VI: BUSINESS COMMUNICATION

---

## 37. Work Behaviour and Communication

Professional communication is part of work performance.

It affects how people judge:

- Expertise
- Maturity
- Authority
- Reliability
- Leadership potential
- Trustworthiness
- Executive presence
- Client readiness

Technical skill is not enough if communication does not carry it.

---

## 38. Impressive Self-Introduction

A self-introduction should be concise, confident, and useful.

## Structure

1. Name
2. Role or function
3. Relevant value
4. Context or purpose

## Example

> “I’m Abe. I lead cybersecurity and IT governance for the region. My focus is keeping operations secure, resilient, and aligned with business risk.”

## Rules

- Do not ramble.
- Do not apologise.
- Do not overload details.
- Match the context.
- Speak clearly.
- Hold eye contact.
- End cleanly.

---

## 39. Intelligent Interview

An interview is not only about answering questions. It is about demonstrating communication maturity.

## Prepare

- Know your core stories.
- Practise aloud.
- Record yourself.
- Use concise answers.
- Avoid overexplaining.
- Speak with steady pace.
- Use examples.
- Ask intelligent questions.

## Interview answer structure

1. Direct answer
2. Brief context
3. Specific example
4. Result
5. Link back to role

## Avoid

- Rambling
- Mumbling
- Fillers
- Defensive tone
- Excessive modesty
- Overclaiming
- Weak endings

---

## 40. Voicemail

Voicemail should be clear and brief.

## Structure

1. Name
2. Reason for call
3. Key information
4. Requested action
5. Callback number
6. Repeat number if needed

## Example

> “Hello, this is Abe Yasser. I’m calling regarding the access review meeting. Please call me back today before 3 p.m. at [number]. Again, that is [number]. Thank you.”

## Rules

- Slow down for names and numbers.
- Do not leave long stories.
- Speak clearly.
- Repeat critical information.
- End politely.

---

## 41. Getting Your Point Across

To get your point across:

- Know your point before speaking.
- State it early.
- Use simple declarative sentences.
- Support with only necessary detail.
- Watch listener response.
- Stop when understood.
- Confirm next action.

## Formula

> Point → Reason → Evidence → Action

Example:

> “We should delay the rollout. The control testing is incomplete. Two critical exceptions remain open. I recommend we close those first and review again Friday.”

---

## 42. Smooth Small Talk

Small talk is not meaningless. It is social lubrication.

It helps people:

- Build comfort
- Establish rapport
- Transition into business
- Reduce tension
- Show warmth
- Open relationships

## Small talk topics

- Setting
- Travel
- Weather
- Shared event
- Work context
- Light personal interests
- Food
- Local environment

## Avoid early

- Politics
- Religion
- Money
- Health details
- Deep personal problems
- Gossip
- Controversial topics

## Techniques

- Use “free information” from what the person says.
- Ask light follow-up questions.
- Offer small personal details.
- Do not interrogate.
- Allow topic shifts.
- Exit gracefully.

## Business bridge

Move from small talk to business with a smooth transition:

> “Good to hear. Shall we look at the agenda?”

---

## 43. Speaking in Front of People

Public speaking creates leadership opportunity but also anxiety.

The solution is skill, not wishing fear away.

## Preparation

- Know your audience.
- Define the objective.
- Organise clearly.
- Rehearse aloud.
- Practise in the room if possible.
- Use notes, not scripts.
- Prepare opening and closing carefully.
- Record practice.
- Get feedback.

## Audience relationship

Speak to people, not at them.

Use:

- Eye contact
- Conversational phrasing
- Clear structure
- Stories
- Pauses
- Vocal projection
- Strong endings

## Common problem

People write for the page and then read to the room.

Better:

- Write for the ear.
- Speak from notes.
- Use shorter sentences.
- Use natural phrasing.
- Keep your face available.

---

## 44. PowerPoint

PowerPoint is a supplement, not the speaker.

## Good PowerPoint

- Supports the message
- Uses minimal text
- Shows visuals when useful
- Clarifies structure
- Does not compete with the speaker
- Is readable from the back
- Leaves space for explanation

## Bad PowerPoint

- Too much text
- Speaker reads slides
- Slides replace thinking
- Visual clutter
- Tiny fonts
- Too many bullet points
- Weak opening
- No clear conclusion

## Noncompete principle

The slide and speaker should not compete.

If the audience is reading dense slides, they are not listening to the speaker.

# PART VII: PRACTICAL TRAINING PLAN

---

## 45. Four-Week Speech Improvement Plan

## Week 1: Assessment

Actions:

- Record 3 natural conversations or simulated responses.
- Record one reading passage.
- Listen for speed, volume, clarity, pitch, and endings.
- Ask two trusted people for feedback.
- Select one priority issue.

Output:

- One clearly defined speech goal.

## Week 2: Controlled practice

Actions:

- Practise 3 to 4 times daily for 6 to 12 minutes.
- Use reading passages.
- Record and listen.
- Focus only on the selected issue.
- Practise slowly and deliberately.

Output:

- Better control in structured practice.

## Week 3: Neutral listener practice

Actions:

- Use the new habit in low-risk real interactions.
- Practise phone calls, store questions, short introductions, and voicemail.
- Keep a simple log.
- Ask for feedback where possible.

Output:

- Carryover into real speech.

## Week 4: Professional application

Actions:

- Apply the new habit in meetings, calls, presentations, and work discussions.
- Record one real or mock presentation.
- Review progress.
- Select next improvement goal.

Output:

- Practical integration.

---

## 46. Daily Practice Menu

Choose one drill per session.

### Rate drill

Read 160 words in about one minute.

### Pause drill

Mark pauses in a paragraph and read aloud.

### Ending drill

Read sentences while keeping energy through the final word.

### Projection drill

Speak to a person or object at the back of the room.

### Resonance drill

Hum gently, feel forward vibration, then speak a phrase.

### Declarative sentence drill

Turn long vague statements into short direct sentences.

### Articulation drill

Practise final consonants and difficult sound combinations.

### Eye contact drill

Deliver one thought to one listener before moving to another.

### Voicemail drill

Leave yourself a professional message and evaluate it.

## 47. Executive Presence Through Voice

Executive presence is communicated by:

- Steady pace
- Clear articulation
- Strong sentence endings
- Controlled pauses
- Adequate volume
- Natural pitch
- Resonance
- Concise language
- Calm posture
- Direct eye contact

## Weak executive signals

- Rushing
- Mumbling
- Fading endings
- Overexplaining
- Excessive qualifiers
- Nervous laughter
- Weak volume
- Reading everything
- Poor eye contact
- Monotone delivery

## Strong executive signals

- “Here is the issue.”
- “This is the risk.”
- “My recommendation is clear.”
- “The decision needed today is this.”
- “There are two options.”
- “I recommend option two.”

---

## 48. Meeting Communication Playbook

Before speaking:

- Know your point.
- Decide whether you are informing, recommending, challenging, or deciding.
- Use a direct opening.
- Speak at listener speed.
- End with action.

## Meeting formula

> Context → Point → Reason → Action

Example:

> “The context is the audit timeline. My point is that we need one owner for evidence collection. Without that, the process will remain fragmented. I recommend assigning ownership today.”

---

## 49. Difficult Conversation Playbook

Use voice control to stay credible.

## Do

- Slow down.
- Lower intensity.
- Use clear statements.
- Keep endings strong.
- Pause before answering.
- Keep posture open.
- Avoid sarcasm.
- Avoid rushing.
- Ask clarifying questions.

## Do not

- Raise volume unnecessarily.
- Let emotion increase speed.
- Overexplain.
- Apologise for your point.
- Speak with fading endings.
- Hide behind vague language.

# PART X: Critical Assessment

## Strengths

- Practical and exercise-based
- Focuses on how speech actually sounds
- Treats communication holistically: voice, words, and body
- Strong emphasis on recording and self-awareness
- Useful for professional communication, not only public speaking
- Gives specific remedies for specific speech issues
- Highlights deliberate practice and carryover into real life
- Good for people who lack confidence in their speaking voice

## Limitations

- Some readers may need a speech-language pathologist rather than self-help exercises.
- The book’s examples are mostly individual coaching cases, so readers must adapt them to their own context.
- Some advice may need cultural adjustment.
- Voice work can be psychological as well as technical, so progress may not be purely mechanical.
- The book assumes strong personal motivation and discipline.
- Serious voice strain, hoarseness, or speech disorders should not be self-treated only through reading.

## Responsible use

Use the book to improve clarity, confidence, professionalism, and connection. Do not use it to erase identity, force an artificial persona, or imitate someone else’s voice. The goal is a stronger version of your own communication.

# PART XI: One-Page Executive Summary

*It’s the Way You Say It* argues that communication impact depends not only on words, but also on voice, speech habits, body language, and delivery. Carol Fleming shows that people often misjudge their own voice because they hear themselves internally and are too focused on meaning to evaluate sound accurately. Therefore, effective improvement begins with recording, listening, external feedback, and clear identification of the specific issue to address.

The book gives practical methods for common speech problems: fast speech, excessive loudness, weak volume, raspy voice, high pitch, indecisive tone, staccato rhythm, breathy voice, and fading endings. It then moves into developing a dynamic voice through rate, pitch, volume, pause, emphasis, resonance, and breath support. The book also addresses becoming well-spoken through simple declarative sentences, precise vocabulary, reduced filler language, gracious responses, articulation, and listener-aware communication.

A major strength of the book is its holistic model: body, words, and voice must communicate the same message at the same time. Confidence is carried not only by what a person says, but by posture, head carriage, facial expression, eye contact, vocal steadiness, and directness. The final business sections apply these skills to self-introductions, interviews, voicemail, small talk, presentations, and PowerPoint.

The practical lesson is clear: speech improvement is possible, but it requires deliberate practice. New behaviours will feel strange before they feel natural. Short, repeated, focused practice, combined with recording and real-world carryover, can change how a person sounds and how others perceive their professionalism, authority, warmth, and confidence.
