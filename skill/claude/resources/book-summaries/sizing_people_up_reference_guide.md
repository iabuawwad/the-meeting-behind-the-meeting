# Sizing People Up  
## Reference Guide, Frameworks, Key Takeaways, Techniques, and Behaviour Prediction Manual

**Book:** *Sizing People Up: A Veteran FBI Agent’s User Manual for Behavior Prediction*  
**Author:** Robin Dreeke and Cameron Stauth  
**Purpose of this guide:** Turn the book into a practical reference for assessing trustworthiness, predicting behaviour, building alliances, avoiding manipulation, and making better decisions about people.

---

## 1. Core Thesis

The book argues that one of the most important life and leadership skills is the ability to predict what people are likely to do.

The goal is not to judge whether someone is morally good or bad. The goal is to assess whether you can reasonably trust that person in a specific situation.

The core message:

> Trust is not a feeling.  
> Trust is predictability.

You can like someone and not trust them.  
You can trust someone for one thing and not another.  
You can trust someone’s intention but not their competence.  
You can trust someone under normal pressure but not under desperation or temptation.

The book replaces intuition with a structured six-sign system for behaviour prediction.

---

## 2. The Central Operating Principle

People usually act in what they believe to be their own best interest.

This is not automatically cynical. Self-interest can include:

- Family protection
- Loyalty
- Reputation
- Money
- Status
- Security
- Mission
- Faith
- Career growth
- Altruism
- Love
- Identity
- Belonging
- Avoiding shame
- Avoiding punishment

The practical question is not:

> “Is this person good?”

The stronger question is:

> “What does this person believe is in their own best interest, and what behaviour is that likely to produce?”

---

## 3. What the Book Is Really Providing

This book provides a decision system for assessing people.

It helps readers:

- Predict whether someone is trustworthy in a specific context
- Reduce overreliance on gut feeling
- Separate liking from trust
- Spot signs of desperation, instability, unreliability, and manipulation
- Build stronger alliances
- Understand people’s motivations
- Verify claims with observable behaviour
- Avoid emotional hijacking
- Make rational relationship, hiring, leadership, and business decisions

The system is built around six signs:

1. Vesting
2. Longevity
3. Reliability
4. Actions
5. Language
6. Stability

# PART I: FOUNDATIONS OF BEHAVIOUR PREDICTION

---

## 4. Trust Comes First

The most common reason to size someone up is to measure trustworthiness.

Trust matters in:

- Hiring
- Leadership
- Business partnerships
- Vendor relationships
- Friendships
- Marriage
- Family
- Intelligence work
- Security
- Negotiation
- Crisis response
- Team building

The book defines trustworthiness as the ability to predict that someone will behave in a way that supports your legitimate expectations.

Trust is not blind faith. It requires evidence.

---

## 5. Trust, But Verify

The book repeatedly uses the principle:

> Trust, but verify.

People may mislead you intentionally.  
People may also mislead you unintentionally because they overestimate themselves, misunderstand reality, or believe their own preferred story.

Verification is not hostility. It is responsible decision-making.

## Verification methods

- Check track record
- Look for current behaviour
- Ask specific questions
- Compare words with actions
- Request evidence
- Talk to credible references
- Observe under pressure
- Test with small commitments first
- Watch response to accountability
- Look for simple, consistent explanations

---

## 6. Keep Feelings Out of the Assessment

Emotion can distort behavioural prediction.

Common errors:

- Trusting someone because you like them
- Distrusting someone because they are socially awkward
- Confusing confidence with competence
- Confusing charm with trustworthiness
- Confusing familiarity with reliability
- Confusing status with integrity
- Confusing similarity with safety
- Confusing fear with evidence

The book’s standard:

> Assess behaviour rationally, objectively, and nonjudgmentally.

This does not mean becoming cold. It means refusing to let fear, attraction, anger, admiration, or hope replace evidence.

---

## 7. The Hard Truths About Predicting Behaviour

## 7.1 Behaviour is variable

People behave differently depending on:

- Desperation
- Temptation
- Punishment risk
- Opportunity
- Incentives
- Pressure
- Accountability
- Relationship duration
- Personal stability
- Perceived consequences

## 7.2 Immunity affects behaviour

When people feel immune from consequences because of wealth, power, status, protection, or organisational politics, behaviour may degrade.

Power can reduce accountability.

## 7.3 Vulnerability can increase predictability

People who face consequences are usually easier to predict than people who are insulated from consequences.

## 7.4 Incompetence is common

People may fail not because they are dishonest, but because they cannot do what they promised.

This is why reliability includes both:

- Competence
- Diligence

## 7.5 Intuition is weak

Gut feeling is common but unreliable, especially when:

- You like the person
- You fear the person
- You need the deal
- You are desperate
- They are charming
- They resemble people you trust
- They trigger your biases

## 7.6 Appearances mislead

The book warns against overvaluing:

- Physical appearance
- Confidence
- Professional title
- Religious or political affiliation
- Social status
- Proximity
- Charisma
- Similarity
- Polished language

These may feel reassuring but are not strong proof of trustworthiness.

## 7.7 Longevity matters

Short relationships are easier to exploit. Long relationships create stronger incentives for cooperation.

## 7.8 Their perception matters more than yours

To predict someone, understand their worldview.

Your view of what they should want is less useful than what they actually believe they want.

## 7.9 Persistence works if it is nonjudgmental

You can pursue truth firmly if the other person feels understood rather than judged.

If they feel judged, they will hide, defend, or retaliate.

---

## 8. Emotional Hijacking

Emotional hijacking happens when fear, anger, urgency, ego, or personal insecurity takes over decision-making.

Symptoms:

- Jumping to unlikely conclusions
- Assuming malicious intent without evidence
- Overreacting
- Trusting too quickly
- Distrusting everyone
- Making decisions to reduce anxiety
- Ignoring obvious explanations
- Creating drama
- Escalating unnecessarily

Corrective principle:

> Think horses, not zebras.

Start with the most likely explanation before chasing extreme scenarios.

---

## 9. Stempathy

The book introduces the idea of **stempathy**, a combination of:

- Stoicism
- Empathy

## Stoicism

The ability to stay rational, steady, and disciplined under pressure.

## Empathy

The ability to understand another person’s perspective, needs, fears, and incentives.

## Stempathy

A calm, rational, empathetic way of assessing people.

It allows you to:

- See reality clearly
- Understand the person’s point of view
- Avoid emotional overreaction
- Ask better questions
- Predict behaviour more accurately
- Stay kind without becoming naive

# PART II: THE SIX SIGNS FOR BEHAVIOUR PREDICTION

---

# 10. Sign #1: Vesting

## Definition

Vesting means the person believes they benefit from your success.

The key question:

> Does this person believe they will benefit if I succeed?

If yes, they have a reason to protect, support, and advance your interests.

## Why it matters

Vesting creates aligned self-interest.

When someone is vested in you:

- Your success reflects well on them
- Your failure harms them
- They gain from helping you
- They are motivated to protect the relationship
- They are more likely to go beyond minimum effort

## Positive tells

- They ask about your goals
- They offer help without immediate reward
- They introduce you to useful people
- They protect your reputation
- They share information that helps you
- They celebrate your progress
- They make your success visible
- They connect their goals to yours
- They give honest warnings
- They invest time, attention, or resources

## Negative tells

- They only engage when they need something
- They avoid your priorities
- They compete with your success
- They take credit but avoid responsibility
- They disappear when you need help
- They show jealousy or quiet resistance
- They frame success as zero-sum
- They benefit more from your dependency than your growth

## How to test vesting

Ask:

- “What would success look like for both of us?”
- “Where do our goals overlap?”
- “How does this help your priorities?”
- “What would make this a win for you?”
- “What do you need from this relationship?”

Then watch whether their behaviour matches the stated alignment.

## Leadership application

Create mutual success.

Do not expect loyalty from people who cannot see how your success also supports theirs.

---

# 11. Sign #2: Longevity

## Definition

Longevity means the person believes the relationship will continue.

The key question:

> Does this person expect to have a long relationship with me?

## Why it matters

Time creates accountability.

When people expect a long relationship:

- They care about reputation
- They expect future consequences
- They are more likely to cooperate
- They may make short-term sacrifices
- They see value in trust-building
- They avoid burning the relationship

Short relationships invite exploitation because consequences are limited.

## Positive tells

- They discuss future cooperation
- They follow up consistently
- They keep contact after the immediate need
- They invest in relationship quality
- They honour small commitments
- They avoid short-term wins that damage trust
- They behave as if they will see you again
- They care about your long-term view of them

## Negative tells

- They rush the deal
- They avoid future commitments
- They resist documentation
- They disappear after getting value
- They treat the relationship as transactional
- They show no concern for future consequences
- They push for irreversible decisions quickly

## How to test longevity

Ask:

- “How do you see this relationship developing over time?”
- “What should we build after this first step?”
- “How do we keep this working six months from now?”
- “What follow-up rhythm makes sense?”

Watch whether they invest beyond the immediate transaction.

## Executive rule

The shorter the expected relationship, the stronger the verification must be.

---

# 12. Sign #3: Reliability

## Definition

Reliability means the person can do what they say they will do and will actually do it.

The key question:

> Can this person deliver, and will they follow through?

Reliability has two parts:

1. Competence
2. Diligence

## Competence

Can they do the task?

Evidence:

- Relevant experience
- Skills
- Track record
- Past results
- Technical ability
- Decision quality
- Problem-solving ability
- Realistic understanding of the work

## Diligence

Will they do the work properly?

Evidence:

- Follow-through
- Discipline
- Responsiveness
- Preparation
- Ownership
- Attention to detail
- Persistence
- Ability to accept boring or difficult tasks

## Positive tells

- They give realistic estimates
- They know what they do not know
- They provide evidence
- They meet small deadlines
- They communicate early when blocked
- They accept accountability
- They do not overpromise
- They ask clarifying questions
- They keep records
- They handle detail without drama

## Negative tells

- Overconfidence without evidence
- Vague claims
- Excuses
- Missed small commitments
- Blaming others
- Poor preparation
- Inability to explain method
- Inflated résumé
- Lack of ownership
- Optimism replacing realism

## How to test reliability

Use small tests before large trust.

Examples:

- Ask for a small deliverable
- Set a short deadline
- Request written summary
- Ask for evidence
- Check references
- Ask about failures
- Ask how they handle obstacles
- Give a slightly inconvenient task

## Critical distinction

Good intention is not reliability.

Someone may want to help but still be unable to deliver.

---

# 13. Sign #4: Actions

## Definition

Actions are consistent patterns of observable behaviour.

The key question:

> Does this person’s behaviour consistently support their claims?

Words matter. Actions matter more.

## Why actions matter

People can tell stories about who they are. Their behaviour shows what they prioritise.

## Positive tells

- Actions match words
- Current behaviour supports stated values
- Recent behaviour is consistent
- They act transparently
- They communicate honestly
- They correct mistakes
- They do what they said without being chased
- They accept responsibility
- They are consistent across settings
- They behave well when it costs them something

## Negative tells

- Words and actions conflict
- They explain too much but do little
- They hide details
- They say one thing to you and another to others
- They are inconsistent under pressure
- They avoid accountability
- They blame circumstances repeatedly
- They exaggerate achievements
- They show secrecy where transparency is expected

## Past behaviour versus current behaviour

Past behaviour matters, but recent behaviour in a similar context matters more.

People change. Situations change. Pressure changes.

Assess:

- Current behaviour
- Recent similar behaviour
- Behaviour under pressure
- Behaviour when accountable
- Behaviour when tempted

## How to test actions

Ask:

- “What did you do when this happened before?”
- “Can you walk me through the steps?”
- “Who else was involved?”
- “What was the result?”
- “What would you do differently now?”
- “What evidence can we review?”

## Executive rule

Behaviour is the audit trail of character.

---

# 14. Sign #5: Language

## Definition

Language reveals thought patterns, emotional control, respect, truthfulness, and social skill.

The key question:

> Does this person communicate in a way that builds trust, clarity, and connection?

## Why language matters

How people speak often reveals how they think and how they handle people.

The book is especially critical of “talking trash.”

## Negative language patterns

- Blaming
- Insults
- Bombast
- Exaggeration
- Manipulation
- Evasiveness
- Hyperemotion
- Rudeness
- Contempt
- Gossip
- Constant criticism
- Debate tactics instead of truth-seeking
- Complexity used to conceal
- Humour used to wound
- “Just being honest” used as cover for disrespect

## Positive language patterns

- Simple explanations
- Respectful directness
- Calm tone
- Ownership
- Specificity
- Accurate wording
- No unnecessary drama
- Questions that seek understanding
- Clear commitments
- Consistency
- Tact
- Rationality
- Empathy
- Plain talk

## Key principle

Simplicity reveals. Complexity can conceal.

This does not mean every complex explanation is deceptive. It means unnecessary complexity deserves attention.

## Useful questions

- “Can you explain that simply?”
- “What exactly happened?”
- “What do you mean by that?”
- “What is the specific commitment?”
- “What part are you responsible for?”
- “What is the simplest version of this?”

## Executive rule

Watch how people speak about people who are not in the room. Eventually, they may speak about you the same way.

---

# 15. Sign #6: Stability

## Definition

Stability means emotional steadiness, internal consistency, and the ability to handle conflict without destructive behaviour.

The key question:

> Can this person stay emotionally regulated enough to remain predictable under pressure?

## Why it matters

Unstable people are hard to predict because their behaviour shifts under stress.

Stability is not lack of emotion. It is the ability to manage emotion.

## Positive tells

- Calm under pressure
- Handles disagreement without escalation
- Accepts feedback
- Recovers after conflict
- Keeps commitments during stress
- Does not weaponise emotion
- Can apologise
- Can pause before reacting
- Maintains proportion
- Separates facts from feelings
- Does not punish people for truth

## Negative tells

- Emotional explosions
- Silent treatment
- Revenge behaviour
- Excessive drama
- Threats
- Unpredictable mood shifts
- Paranoia without evidence
- Overreaction to minor issues
- Inability to handle criticism
- Constant victim narrative
- Using fear, guilt, or chaos to control others
- Creating conflict and then denying it

## How to test stability

Observe:

- How they handle being told no
- How they respond to delay
- How they treat service staff
- How they react to mistakes
- How they describe past conflicts
- Whether they accept responsibility
- Whether they escalate or de-escalate
- Whether their mood controls the room

## Executive rule

Do not build critical dependency on someone whose emotional state you cannot reasonably predict.

# PART III: THE SIX-SIGN TRUST ASSESSMENT MODEL

---

## 16. Behaviour Prediction Scorecard

Rate each sign from 1 to 5.

1 = weak  
2 = concerning  
3 = mixed  
4 = strong  
5 = excellent

| Sign | Question | Score |
|---|---|---|
| Vesting | Do they benefit from my success? |  |
| Longevity | Do they expect a long relationship? |  |
| Reliability | Can and will they deliver? |  |
| Actions | Do actions match words? |  |
| Language | Do they communicate constructively? |  |
| Stability | Are they emotionally predictable under pressure? |  |

## Interpretation

| Total Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 26 to 30 | Strong trust candidate |
| 21 to 25 | Generally trustworthy, verify weak areas |
| 16 to 20 | Mixed, limit exposure and test further |
| 10 to 15 | High caution |
| Below 10 | Do not rely on them for important matters |

## Important note

A single critical weakness can override a strong total score.

For example:

- High competence but low honesty
- Strong relationship but low reliability
- Great language but unstable behaviour
- Long relationship but no vesting
- Charming style but conflicting actions

---

## 17. Dealbreaker Matrix

Some issues require immediate caution.

| Red Flag | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| Repeated lying | Destroys predictability |
| No accountability | Makes correction impossible |
| Words and actions conflict | Shows low integrity or low self-awareness |
| Chronic blame | Prevents learning |
| Emotional volatility | Makes behaviour unstable |
| False competence | Creates delivery risk |
| Short-term exploitation | Signals no longevity |
| Hidden agenda | Breaks trust foundation |
| Trash talk | Reveals poor language and respect |
| Desperation plus opportunity | Raises betrayal risk |
| Immunity from consequences | Reduces behavioural constraints |

# PART IV: PRACTICAL APPLICATIONS

---

## 18. Hiring

Use the six signs to assess candidates.

## Vesting

Does the candidate see mutual success with the role and company?

Ask:

- “What would make this role a win for you and for us?”

## Longevity

Is the candidate thinking beyond the immediate job?

Ask:

- “What would keep you engaged here for the next three years?”

## Reliability

Can they do the work?

Ask:

- “Walk me through a project where you had to deliver under pressure.”

## Actions

Do examples match claims?

Ask:

- “What did you personally do?”

## Language

Do they communicate clearly, respectfully, and simply?

Watch for blame-heavy stories.

## Stability

Can they discuss failure without defensiveness?

Ask:

- “Tell me about a serious mistake and how you handled it.”

---

## 19. Vendor and Partner Assessment

Before trusting a vendor or partner:

- Check whether their incentives align with your success.
- Look beyond sales language.
- Test small commitments.
- Require evidence.
- Assess delivery history.
- Watch how they handle objections.
- Clarify the long-term relationship model.
- Ask for current comparable references.
- Verify who will actually do the work.

## Strong question

> “How do you make money if we succeed, and what happens if we fail?”

This exposes vesting.

---

## 20. Leadership and Team Management

Leaders must assess who can be trusted with:

- Confidential information
- Critical tasks
- Client relationships
- Escalations
- Security responsibilities
- Financial decisions
- Team influence
- Crisis response

## Use the six signs

- Vesting: Do they care about team success?
- Longevity: Are they building long-term value?
- Reliability: Can they deliver?
- Actions: Do they behave consistently?
- Language: Do they communicate constructively?
- Stability: Can they handle pressure?

## Leadership rule

Do not promote only based on competence. Promote based on predictable trustworthiness.

---

## 21. Personal Relationships

The book’s model applies beyond business.

Ask:

- Does this person benefit from my well-being?
- Do they see a future with me?
- Are they reliable?
- Do their actions match their words?
- Do they use respectful language?
- Are they emotionally stable?

Hard truth:

> Love is not enough to make someone trustworthy.

Trust must still be verified through behaviour.

---

## 22. Security and Insider Risk

For cybersecurity, fraud, and insider risk, the model is useful but must be applied carefully and ethically.

Risk indicators may include:

- Desperation
- Grievance
- Unusual secrecy
- Declining reliability
- Hostile language
- Instability
- Isolation
- Access plus motive
- Short-term relationship mindset
- Immunity from consequences
- Behaviour change under pressure

Do not turn this into amateur profiling.

Use it to trigger proper review, documentation, support, and control validation.

---

## 23. Negotiation

In negotiation, assess:

- Does the other side gain from your success?
- Are they likely to work with you again?
- Can they deliver what they promise?
- Are they acting consistently?
- Is their language clear or evasive?
- Do they stay stable under pressure?

## Practical rule

Do not negotiate only the deal. Negotiate the predictability of the person.

---

## 24. Crisis Situations

Under crisis, behaviour changes.

People may:

- Freeze
- Overreact
- Hide
- Help
- Blame
- Take charge
- Collapse
- Protect others
- Exploit confusion

The book’s opening 9/11 story is used to show that even familiar people can become unpredictable under extreme pressure.

Crisis reveals:

- Stability
- Vesting
- Courage
- Reliability
- Self-interest
- Emotional regulation
- Real priorities

# PART V: OBSERVATION AND QUESTIONING TECHNIQUES

---

## 25. Data Points to Collect

Assess people through multiple data sources:

- What they say
- What they do
- What others say about them
- Their current behaviour
- Their recent track record
- Their response to accountability
- Their behaviour under stress
- Their incentives
- Their emotional patterns
- Their communication style
- Their treatment of lower-status people
- Their consistency across settings

## Rule

One data point is not enough.

---

## 26. Strong Behaviour Prediction Questions

## Vesting

- “What does success look like for you here?”
- “How does my success affect your goals?”
- “What would make this mutually valuable?”

## Longevity

- “How do you see this relationship evolving?”
- “What should we build after the first result?”
- “What would make this worth continuing?”

## Reliability

- “What similar work have you done?”
- “What evidence can you show?”
- “What could stop you from delivering?”
- “Where might you need support?”

## Actions

- “What did you actually do?”
- “What happened next?”
- “Who can confirm that?”
- “What changed because of your action?”

## Language

- “Can you explain that in plain terms?”
- “What exactly are you committing to?”
- “What do you mean by that?”

## Stability

- “How do you handle conflict?”
- “Tell me about a time you were strongly challenged.”
- “What happens when people disagree with you?”
- “What mistake taught you the most?”

---

## 27. How to Listen

Listen for:

- Specificity
- Simplicity
- Ownership
- Consistency
- Emotional tone
- Blame
- Exaggeration
- Defensiveness
- Evasion
- Contradiction
- Unrealistic certainty
- Respect for others
- Awareness of consequences

---

## 28. How to Verify Without Creating Resistance

Use nonjudgmental language.

Instead of:

> “I don’t believe you.”

Say:

> “I want to understand this clearly. What evidence should we review?”

Instead of:

> “That sounds suspicious.”

Say:

> “Help me reconcile this with what happened earlier.”

Instead of:

> “You failed.”

Say:

> “Walk me through where the plan broke down.”

Instead of:

> “Why did you hide this?”

Say:

> “What made this difficult to raise earlier?”

# PART VI: COMMON MISTAKES

---

## 29. Mistake 1: Confusing trust with morality

Trust is context-specific predictability.

A person can be moral but unreliable.  
A person can be competent but selfish.  
A person can be kind but unstable.  
A person can be honest but unable to deliver.

---

## 30. Mistake 2: Trusting confidence

Confidence is presentation. Reliability is evidence.

Always ask:

- What have they done?
- Can they repeat it?
- Under what conditions?
- Who verified it?
- What failed?
- What did they learn?

---

## 31. Mistake 3: Ignoring desperation

Desperation changes behaviour.

People may do things they normally would not do when facing:

- Financial collapse
- Public shame
- Career loss
- Family pressure
- Legal exposure
- Addiction
- Fear
- Status loss
- Personal crisis

Desperation does not make someone bad, but it raises prediction risk.

---

## 32. Mistake 4: Believing similarity equals trust

Shared background, culture, interests, or values can create comfort. It does not prove trustworthiness.

Similarity is a comfort signal, not a proof signal.

---

## 33. Mistake 5: Ignoring small commitments

Small commitments are behavioural samples.

Someone who repeatedly misses small commitments may not be safe with large commitments.

---

## 34. Mistake 6: Overlooking language

People often reveal future behaviour in how they speak.

Warning signs:

- “Everyone else is stupid.”
- “They forced me.”
- “Rules are for people who do not know how to win.”
- “Trust me.”
- “It is complicated.”
- “No one needs to know.”
- “I always deliver,” with no evidence.
- “That was not my fault,” repeatedly.

# PART VII: BEHAVIOUR PREDICTION PLAYBOOKS

---

## 35. New Relationship Playbook

Use this when meeting a new person for business or personal reasons.

1. Identify what they want.
2. Determine whether your success helps them.
3. Assess expected relationship duration.
4. Test reliability through small commitments.
5. Compare words and actions.
6. Listen to language patterns.
7. Observe emotional stability.
8. Verify important claims.
9. Increase trust gradually.
10. Keep exposure proportional to evidence.

---

## 36. Existing Relationship Review

Use this for employees, partners, friends, or vendors.

Ask:

- Has reliability improved or declined?
- Are actions still matching words?
- Has language become more negative?
- Is there new desperation?
- Has the relationship become short-term?
- Are incentives still aligned?
- Has emotional stability changed?
- What recent evidence matters most?

---

## 37. High-Stakes Decision Checklist

Before trusting someone with a high-stakes matter:

- [ ] I know what they want.
- [ ] Their success is linked to my success.
- [ ] They expect an ongoing relationship.
- [ ] They have demonstrated competence.
- [ ] They have demonstrated diligence.
- [ ] Their actions match their claims.
- [ ] Their language is clear and respectful.
- [ ] They are stable under pressure.
- [ ] I have verified key claims.
- [ ] I have tested small commitments.
- [ ] I have considered desperation and temptation.
- [ ] I have limited exposure if uncertainty remains.

---

## 38. Trust Boundary Framework

Trust should be specific, not unlimited.

Examples:

- “I trust him to deliver technical work, but not to manage clients.”
- “I trust her judgement, but she needs support on deadlines.”
- “I trust this vendor for low-risk support, not critical infrastructure.”
- “I trust his intent, but not his execution.”
- “I trust her under normal pressure, but not during conflict.”

This prevents all-or-nothing thinking.

# PART VIII: LEADERSHIP APPLICATION

---

## 39. Build Trustworthy Teams

A leader should build teams where members are predictable in positive ways.

This requires:

- Clear expectations
- Aligned incentives
- Long-term relationship signals
- Accountability
- Competence development
- Behavioural standards
- Respectful language
- Emotional discipline
- Verification without paranoia
- Consequences for repeated violations

## Leadership principle

Trust is not created by telling people to trust each other. It is created by consistent, verifiable behaviour.

---

## 40. Create Vesting

Make team members see how success is shared.

Actions:

- Connect individual goals to team outcomes
- Share credit
- Create mutual wins
- Remove zero-sum competition
- Reward collaboration
- Explain how each person benefits from team success

---

## 41. Increase Longevity

Create long-term orientation.

Actions:

- Discuss future growth
- Build development plans
- Create continuity
- Honour relationships after projects end
- Avoid purely transactional management
- Make people feel their reputation matters over time

---

## 42. Strengthen Reliability

Actions:

- Define competence clearly
- Train people
- Use checklists
- Track commitments
- Clarify ownership
- Give feedback early
- Match responsibility to capability
- Test before expanding authority

---

## 43. Reinforce Actions

Actions:

- Reward behaviour, not claims
- Make accountability visible
- Review recent evidence
- Use after-action reviews
- Require proof of completion
- Address inconsistency quickly

---

## 44. Improve Language

Actions:

- Ban contempt as a management style
- Require plain explanations
- Encourage respectful disagreement
- Stop gossip early
- Model calm directness
- Teach people to raise risk without drama

---

## 45. Maintain Stability

Actions:

- Reward emotional discipline
- Do not tolerate intimidation
- Teach conflict skills
- Separate facts from emotions
- Provide support during stress
- Address volatility before promotion
- Protect the team from chaos-makers

# PART X: Critical Assessment

## Strengths

- Practical trust-assessment model
- Clear six-sign framework
- Useful for leadership, hiring, partnerships, security, and personal relationships
- Strong warning against relying on intuition
- Separates liking from trust
- Emphasises verification and evidence
- Recognises competence as part of trust
- Highlights emotional stability as a predictor
- Encourages empathy without naivety

## Limitations

- Some examples are intelligence and FBI-oriented, so readers must adapt the method to normal business and personal settings.
- The “self-interest” principle is useful but can be overapplied if interpreted too cynically.
- Behaviour prediction is probabilistic, not certain.
- The framework depends on access to enough behavioural data.
- Cultural context can affect language, emotional expression, and relationship-building.
- The model may be misused if applied as suspicion rather than disciplined assessment.
- It does not replace formal due diligence, HR process, legal review, psychological assessment, or security investigation.

## Responsible use

Use the model to improve judgment, not to label people permanently.

A person may be untrustworthy for one role but trustworthy for another.  
A person may be unreliable now but improve with support.  
A person may be stable in one context and unstable in another.  
A person may have good intent but poor capacity.

The goal is accurate boundaries, not condemnation.

# PART XI: One-Page Executive Summary

*Sizing People Up* teaches that the core skill in assessing people is behaviour prediction. Robin Dreeke argues that trust should not be treated as a feeling, moral assumption, or gut instinct. Trust is predictability: the ability to reasonably forecast what someone will do in a specific situation.

The book’s foundation is that people usually act in what they believe to be their own best interest. To predict behaviour, you must understand what the other person wants, fears, values, protects, and expects. This requires rational analysis, empathy, and emotional discipline. Dreeke calls this blend “stempathy”: stoicism plus empathy.

The book’s six signs of behaviour prediction are vesting, longevity, reliability, actions, language, and stability. Vesting asks whether the person benefits from your success. Longevity asks whether they expect an ongoing relationship. Reliability asks whether they can and will do what they promise. Actions ask whether behaviour matches claims. Language asks whether communication builds trust or reveals manipulation, blame, and disrespect. Stability asks whether the person remains emotionally predictable under pressure.

The book warns against trusting appearances, intuition, confidence, similarity, charm, status, or likeability. It also warns that desperation, temptation, immunity from consequences, and short-term relationships can distort behaviour. Trust should therefore be verified through evidence, current behaviour, small commitments, consistent actions, and clear communication.

The practical lesson is simple: do not ask whether someone is good or bad. Ask what they are likely to do, why they are likely to do it, what evidence supports that prediction, and how much trust the evidence justifies.
